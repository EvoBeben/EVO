01 Sequence

Base and lean are two different jobs

You asked to get in shape, build a base, then get lean and muscular. Those are three jobs and they want three different sets of instructions — trying to do all of them at once is the single most common reason a first year of training produces nothing. The order below is the plan. It runs about twelve months.

Phase 1

Base

Weeks 1–8

Learn the eight movement patterns and build the habit. Volume is deliberately below the research optimum — you are training your technique and your tolerance, not your muscles. Eat at maintenance. Do not diet.

Phase 2

Build

Weeks 9–24

Add a set to every main movement, which lifts you into the evidence-backed volume band. Eat at maintenance or slightly above. This is where most of your muscle gets built, and where you'll start climbing the Load Ladder as the backpack runs out.

Phase 3

Reveal

Weeks 25+

Hold the training exactly as it is and drop calories 10–20%. You cannot reveal muscle you haven't built, which is why this phase is last. Protein goes up, training volume does not come down.

The scale is going to lie to you, and that's expected. Untrained people are the one group who reliably gain muscle and lose fat at the same time. In a 2026 trial at a 500 kcal/day deficit, men lost 8.9 kg of fat while gaining 0.8 kg of lean mass over about five months; women lost 6.4 kg of fat and gained 0.9 kg of lean mass. Beginner men in year one typically add roughly 0.7–1.0 kg of muscle per month.

Those two changes cancel out on a bathroom scale. Measure your waist, take photos in the same light every four weeks, and watch your lift numbers. Weigh yourself if you like, but don't let it be the thing that tells you whether this is working.

04 Context

Why there are no dumbbells in this plan

Yours went back under the recall, and that was the right call. On 5 June 2025 the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission announced a recall of approximately 3,844,200 BowFlex adjustable dumbbells — models 552 (5–52.5 lb) and 1090 (10–90 lb), sold 2004 through May 2025. The weight plates can dislodge from the handle during use: roughly 350 reports, 111 injuries, including concussions, broken toes, abrasions and contusions. If you haven't filed yet, do it at bowflex.com/dumbbell-recalls.html or on 800-209-3539.

So this plan assumes you have none, and it is not a holding pattern. Two findings make that a real claim rather than a consolation. First: light loads taken close to failure build muscle just as well as heavy loads — only maximal one-rep strength clearly favours heavy weight. Second: when push-ups were matched to a bench press for load over 8 weeks, chest growth was 18.3% versus 19.4% and triceps 9.5% versus 10.3% — differences too small to be statistically meaningful.

What you actually give up is maximal strength progress and a degree of comfort, because a fixed load means every set has to go closer to failure. What you keep is the muscle. When replacements arrive, the dumbbell version of this program is archived at the end of the program section — nothing has been thrown away.

02 Fitness

Seven things that decide whether this works

Almost everything else people argue about is noise. These seven carry the result.

Progressive overload

non-negotiable

The one rule with no exceptions: over time, the work has to get harder. More weight, more reps, more sets, better range. If nothing on your log went up this month, nothing on your body will either.

Effort

0–2 RIR

RIR = reps in reserve, how many you could still have done. Anywhere from 0 to about 4 produces comparable growth with heavy weights — but with a fixed light load you need the lower end of that range. Sets stopped more than 5 reps short consistently underperform.

Load

doesn't matter much

The finding this whole plan rests on: light loads taken to failure build muscle just as well as heavy ones. Only maximal one-rep strength clearly favours heavy weight. Push-ups matched to a bench press grew pecs 18.3% vs 19.4% over 8 weeks — not a significant difference.

Volume

10–20 sets

Per muscle, per week. Ten or more beats fewer than ten; the productive range runs to about twenty, with clear diminishing returns above that. Adding a set beats grinding an existing set closer to failure.

Frequency

2× per muscle

Every muscle, at least twice a week. A 3-day full-body split gives you three exposures automatically — which is the main reason it's the right choice for you rather than a body-part split.

Rest between sets

2–3 min

Counter-intuitive but well tested: 3 minutes beat 1 minute for both strength and size over 8 weeks. Long rests aren't wasted time, they're why six exercises fill an hour. Short rests are for the accessory pairs only.

Range of motion

full, stretched

Use the whole range, and prioritise the stretched end. Recent work suggests the lengthened position drives a disproportionate share of growth — training partials in the stretch produced adaptations similar to full range.

On the guidelines themselves. The American College of Sports Medicine updated its resistance training position stand in 2026, its first revision in seventeen years, and the headline change was a relaxation: it dropped the old minimum-dose rules. There is no longer a threshold you must clear for training to "count." The largest single improvement available to anyone is going from no resistance training to some. Everything above is about optimising — it is not a gate you have to pass to start.

03 The program

Three sessions, rotated

Run A, B, C in order with at least one rest day between sessions — Monday / Wednesday / Friday is the usual answer. They rotate rather than being numbered, because no session depends on the one before it. Miss a Wednesday and you do B on Thursday; you don't restart anything.

Every session is four main movements at 2–3 minutes rest, then one superset pair at 60–75 seconds. Tap any exercise for setup, execution, and the two faults that actually matter.

You need one thing that isn't in the house: a backpack you don't mind ruining. Fill it with books, rice, sand, or water bottles — a 1-litre bottle is almost exactly a kilo, which makes them the easiest thing to count. Pack the heavy items low and hard against the back panel so nothing shifts mid-rep, and tighten the straps until it doesn't move. That pack is the only adjustable resistance you have, so treat it as equipment rather than an afterthought.

Everything else is your bench, your floor, a latched door, a towel, and two stacks of thick books.

Week one: find the right version, not the right weight. With bodyweight the variable isn't how much you load — it's which variation you pick. On your first session, choose the easiest version of each movement that still makes 10 reps feel like work: hands high on the bench for push-ups, feet well back for rows, bodyweight only on every leg movement. You should finish every set with 3 or 4 reps left. That will feel far too easy. Do it anyway — week one exists to collect data and teach your joints the patterns, and starting too hard is the most common way a first month ends in an injury or a fortnight of soreness.

Then use double progression — this is the everyday rule that runs the whole plan. Stay on the same variation until you hit the top of the rep range on every set. Then make it harder by one step — more weight in the pack, or the next rung of the Load Ladder — and expect to fall back to the bottom of the range. Then climb again. For example on push-ups: 3 × 8 with hands on the bench → work up to 3 × 12 → move your hands to a lower surface → back to 3 × 8 → climb again → eventually feet on the bench → eventually with the backpack on.

One thing changes because you have no weights. With a fixed load you have to work closer to failure to get the same result — that's why most sets here say 0–2 reps in reserve rather than 2–3. Light loads build muscle just as well as heavy ones, but only when the set is genuinely hard. This is the real cost of training without dumbbells: not less muscle, just less comfort.

Your bench is doing more work now than it was before. Without dumbbells it stops being somewhere to lie down and becomes the main way you change difficulty.

Flat: the platform for step-ups, hip thrusts, bench dips, and the elevation that makes push-ups either easier (hands on it) or harder (feet on it). Incline, ~30°: chest support for the prone Y and T raises, which are the only direct rear-delt and lower-trap work available to you. Decline: loaded sit-ups in Phase 2 — core is otherwise the one slot with no way to progress, since bodyweight is bodyweight forever.

And the bench is what makes single-leg work possible, which matters more here than it did with dumbbells. A rear foot on the bench turns a squat you could do fifty of into a split squat you can barely do ten of. That's the whole trick: when you can't add weight, take a leg away.

Session A — split squat & decline push-up

16 working sets · ~55 min
1 Bulgarian split squat RIR 1–2 › 3 × 8–12 / siderest 2 minquads, glutesbackpack
Setup
  • Rear foot on the bench, laces down. Front foot far enough forward that the front shin stays near-vertical at the bottom.
  • Backpack worn on your back, straps tight so it doesn't swing.
  • Bodyweight only for the first week — get the foot distance right before you load it.
Execution
  • Drop straight down, rear knee toward the floor, torso slightly forward.
  • Descend until the front thigh is at or below parallel.
  • Drive through the front heel. The back leg is a kickstand, not an engine.
Common faults
  • Stance too short, so the front knee shoots forward and the quad cramps. Step further out.
  • Pushing off the back foot. Weight stays about 80/20 on the front leg.

This is now your heaviest leg exercise, and it's carrying the program. One leg at a time halves the load you need, which is exactly why a backpack is enough — and a 2025 meta-analysis found no significant hypertrophy difference between unilateral and bilateral training. Load the pack until 12 reps is genuinely hard.

2 Decline push-up RIR 0–2 › 3 × 8–12rest 2–3 minchest, triceps, front deltsfeet on bench
Setup
  • Feet up on the bench, hands slightly wider than shoulders on the floor.
  • Body in one line from heels to head. Squeeze glutes and brace so the hips don't sag.
  • Backpack worn high on the upper back once bodyweight gets easy.
Execution
  • Lower until your chest is just off the floor — full stretch across the chest.
  • Elbows at about 45° from your sides, not flared to 90°.
  • Press up without letting the hips lead or lag.
Common faults
  • Hips sagging, which turns it into a lower-back exercise. Brace harder or drop to a lower elevation.
  • Half reps. If you can't reach the floor, regress to hands-on-bench until you can.

Too hard right now? Flip it — hands on the bench, feet on the floor, and walk the elevation down over the weeks. That's the whole progression: hands high → hands low → floor → feet on bench → backpack.

3 Towel door row RIR 1–2 › 3 × 10–12rest 90 slats, mid-back, biceps
Setup
  • Find a door that opens away from you. Close and latch it — never use an unlatched door.
  • Throw a strong towel over the top of the door, or loop it around both handles, and grip one end in each hand.
  • Feet close to the door, arms straight, lean back until your weight is on your hands.
Execution
  • Pull the shoulder blades down and back first, then bend the elbows and row your chest toward the door.
  • Lower all the way to straight arms and let the shoulders reach forward.
  • Make it harder by walking your feet further forward so your body angle is lower.
Common faults
  • Hips sagging or piking. Stay in one straight line, same as a plank.
  • Using a flimsy towel or a door that isn't latched. Test it with slow, gentle load before you commit your bodyweight.

If you have a sturdy table or desk, an inverted row underneath it is better — more stable, easier to load by angle. Use that instead if it's available. Either way, see open item 2: pulling is the weak point of this setup and it's worth knowing that.

4 Single-leg Romanian deadlift RIR 1–2 › 3 × 8–10 / siderest 90 shamstrings, glutesbackpack
Setup
  • Backpack held against your chest, or worn on your back. Soft knee on the standing leg.
  • Free hand on the bench for balance while you're learning — that's not cheating.
Execution
  • Hinge at the hip, rear leg extending straight behind you as a counterweight.
  • Hips stay square to the floor — don't let the rear hip roll open.
  • Stop at a strong hamstring stretch, then drive the hip forward to stand.
Common faults
  • Rotating the hips open, which removes most of the hamstring loading.
  • Rounding the back to chase depth instead of stopping where control ends.
5a Prone Y-raise RIR 0–1 › 2 × 12–15supersetrear delts, lower trapsincline bench
Setup
  • Bench at 30°, lie face down with your chest on the pad, arms hanging straight down.
  • No weight at all to start. Your arms are heavier than you think in this position.
Execution
  • Raise both arms overhead into a Y shape, thumbs up, until they're in line with your torso.
  • Hold the top for a full second, then lower slowly.
Common faults
  • Shrugging — the point is the lower traps and rear delts, not the upper traps. Keep the shoulders away from the ears.
  • Rushing. This one is about position and control, not speed.

This earns its place because it's one of the few things a bench gives you that dumbbells were never doing well anyway — direct lower-trap and rear-delt work, which is exactly what counteracts a desk posture. Add a light water bottle in each hand when bodyweight gets easy.

5b Backpack curl RIR 0–1 › 2 × 10–15supersetbiceps
Setup
  • Grip the backpack by the two shoulder straps, one in each hand, pack hanging in front of your thighs.
  • Elbows pinned at your sides for the whole set.
Execution
  • Curl the pack up, pause at the top, lower all the way to straight arms.
  • Because the load is fixed, take these close to failure — 0–1 reps left.
Common faults
  • Swinging the pack up with the hips. If you're rocking, empty some weight out.
  • Elbows drifting forward, turning it into a front raise.

Superset: alternate 5a and 5b back to back with ~60 s between pairs. They don't compete for the same muscles, so this costs nothing and saves about six minutes.

Session B — step-up & pike push-up

16 working sets · ~55 min
1 Step-up RIR 1–2 › 3 × 8–12 / siderest 2 minquads, glutesbackpack
Setup
  • Bench flat, set at a height where your thigh is roughly parallel when your foot is on it.
  • Backpack worn tight. Stand close enough that you step up, not forward.
Execution
  • Plant the whole foot on the bench and drive up through that heel until the leg is straight.
  • Lower under control — resist all the way down rather than dropping.
  • Finish all reps on one side, then switch.
Common faults
  • Pushing off the trailing foot for momentum. The bottom leg should do nothing.
  • Dropping down instead of lowering. The eccentric is half the exercise.

If the bench is too high to reach parallel without hiking your hip, use a lower step until your hip mobility catches up.

2 Pike push-up RIR 0–2 › 3 × 6–10rest 2–3 mindelts, triceps, upper chestfeet on bench
Setup
  • Feet on the bench, hands on the floor, hips pushed high so your body makes an upside-down V.
  • The more vertical your torso, the more this becomes a true overhead press.
Execution
  • Lower the top of your head toward the floor just in front of your hands.
  • Keep the hips high throughout — don't let it collapse into a decline push-up.
  • Press back up until the arms are straight.
Common faults
  • Hips dropping as you fatigue, which quietly turns it into a chest exercise.
  • Head going straight down between the hands instead of forward of them — that's a shoulder-jamming path.

Too hard? Start with feet on the floor in a downward-dog position and walk them closer to your hands over the weeks. Too easy? Wear the backpack, then elevate the bench higher.

3 Backpack one-arm row RIR 1–2 › 3 × 10–12 / siderest 90 slats, mid-back, biceps
Setup
  • Opposite hand and knee on the bench, back flat and roughly parallel to the floor.
  • Grip the backpack by its top handle or by both straps gathered together, hanging straight down.
Execution
  • Pull the shoulder blade down and back, then bend the elbow and row toward your hip.
  • Lower until the shoulder reaches toward the floor. That stretch is the point.
Common faults
  • Twisting the torso to help the pack up. If you're rotating, take some weight out.
  • Stopping short at the bottom. Full reach every rep.

Pack the heavy items low and tight against the back panel so the load doesn't swing mid-rep. A gym-style duffel or a sturdy tote works just as well if the straps are the limiting factor.

4 Hip thrust RIR 1–2 › 3 × 10–15rest 90 sglutes, hamstringsbackpack
Setup
  • Upper back against the long edge of the bench, feet flat, shins vertical at the top.
  • Backpack across the hip crease, held with both hands. A folded towel underneath helps.
Execution
  • Drive the hips up until the torso is parallel to the floor. Ribs stay down.
  • Hold the top for a beat, then lower under control without resting on the floor.
Common faults
  • Hyperextending the lower back at the top instead of finishing with the glutes.
  • Feet too far out, which turns it into a hamstring exercise.

When the pack gets light: switch to the single-leg hip thrust. Same setup, one foot planted, other knee pulled toward your chest. That halves the load requirement and buys you months.

5a Bench dip RIR 0–1 › 2 × 10–15supersettriceps
Setup
  • Hands on the bench edge behind you, fingers forward, legs out in front with heels on the floor.
  • Keep your back close to the bench the whole way — drifting forward is what wrecks shoulders here.
Execution
  • Lower until the upper arms are roughly parallel to the floor. No deeper.
  • Press back up by straightening the elbows, not by shrugging.
Common faults
  • Going too deep and letting the shoulders roll forward. Parallel is the floor of the movement.
  • Bending the knees to cheat. Straighten the legs to make it harder, bend them only to regress.

If your shoulders complain at all, drop this and do a close-grip push-up instead — same triceps job, much friendlier joint position. Add the backpack on your lap to load it.

5b Dead bug RIR 2 › 2 × 8–10 / sidesupersetdeep core→ decline sit-up in Phase 2
Setup
  • On your back, arms straight up, hips and knees bent to 90°.
  • Press the lower back flat into the floor and keep it there. That pressure is the exercise.
Execution
  • Slowly extend the opposite arm and leg toward the floor, breathing out.
  • Return and switch. Stop the rep the instant your back lifts off the floor.
Common faults
  • Letting the lower back arch — you've extended further than you can control.
  • Rushing. Slow is the whole point here.

Phase 2 progression — decline weighted sit-up. Once you can hold the lower back flat through all 10 reps per side without thinking about it, swap this for a decline sit-up: set the bench to a shallow decline, hook your feet, and hold the backpack against your chest. Same 2–3 sets, 10–15 reps.

Do them in this order rather than the reverse. Dead bugs teach you to brace against extension, and that's the exact skill your Romanian deadlift and split squat depend on. Loading trunk flexion before you can brace is how people find their lower back.

Session C — reverse lunge & deficit push-up

16 working sets · ~55 min
1 Reverse lunge RIR 1–2 › 3 × 8–12 / siderest 2 minquads, glutesbackpack
Setup
  • Backpack worn tight, standing tall, feet hip-width.
  • Reverse rather than forward — far kinder to the knees and easier to balance under load.
Execution
  • Step back and drop the rear knee toward the floor, torso upright.
  • Drive through the front heel to return. Finish all reps on one side, then switch.
Common faults
  • Short step, which jams the front knee forward.
  • Leaning over the front thigh instead of staying stacked.

When the pack maxes out: put the front foot on a book or low step. The deficit lets you descend further and loads the stretched position, which is worth more than another five pounds.

2 Deficit push-up RIR 0–2 › 3 × 8–12rest 2–3 minchest, tricepshands elevated
Setup
  • Hands on two stacks of thick books, or two low blocks, roughly 10–15 cm high and shoulder-width apart.
  • Feet on the floor for now — this is already harder than a normal push-up.
  • Test the stacks are stable with slow pressure before committing your weight.
Execution
  • Lower until your chest drops below the level of your hands. That extra range is the entire point.
  • Pause for a beat at the bottom, then press up.
Common faults
  • Not actually using the deficit — sinking to hand level and stopping. If you're not going below, you've just done a harder-to-set-up push-up.
  • Shoulders rolling forward at the bottom. Back off the depth until your mobility catches up.

Why this is in the program: it's the push-up equivalent of rung 4 on the Load Ladder — you can't add plates, so you add range. Recent work suggests the lengthened position drives a disproportionate share of growth, which makes deficit work one of the best tools you have without weights.

3 Towel door row, underhand RIR 1–2 › 3 × 10–12rest 90 slats, biceps
Setup
  • Same setup as Session A — latched door, towel over the top, feet forward.
  • Palms facing up this time, hands closer together.
Execution
  • Pull toward your lower ribs rather than your chest, elbows tucked close to your sides.
  • The underhand grip shifts work toward the lats and biceps compared with Session A's version.
Common faults
  • Hips sagging. One straight line, heels to head.
  • Cutting the range short at the bottom because the angle is too aggressive. Walk your feet back.
4 Bench-anchored hamstring curl RIR 0–1 › 3 × 5–8rest 2 minhamstringseccentric-focused
Setup
  • Kneel on something padded with your heels hooked securely under the bench. Test the bench won't tip before trusting it.
  • Body in one straight line from knees to head, glutes squeezed.
  • Hands up in front of your chest, ready to catch you.
Execution
  • Lower yourself forward as slowly as you possibly can, resisting the whole way with the hamstrings.
  • Catch yourself with your hands, then push back to the start. The lowering is the rep.
  • Start with just 3–5 reps. This is the most humbling exercise on the page.
Common faults
  • Breaking at the hips instead of staying in one line — that removes the hamstring entirely.
  • Doing too many too soon. This causes serious soreness; 3 sets of 5 is plenty for weeks.

Go carefully with this one. It's the strongest hamstring exercise available without weights, and also the easiest to overdo. If you can't control the descent at all, regress: put your hands on the bench in front of you and take some weight through them.

5a Prone T-raise RIR 0–1 › 2 × 12–20supersetrear delts, mid-backincline bench
Setup
  • Bench at 30°, chest on the pad, arms hanging straight down.
  • Thumbs pointing up, small bend in the elbows.
Execution
  • Raise the arms straight out to the sides into a T, squeezing the shoulder blades together.
  • Hold a second at the top, lower slowly.
Common faults
  • Shrugging toward the ears instead of squeezing the blades together.
  • Adding weight too early. Bodyweight arms are genuinely enough here at first.
5b Backpack curl, neutral grip RIR 0–1 › 2 × 10–15supersetbiceps, forearms
Setup
  • Grip the backpack by its top handle with both hands, or by the straps gathered into one bundle.
  • Elbows pinned at your sides.
Execution
  • Curl straight up keeping the grip neutral, lower to full extension every rep.
  • Take these close to failure — the load is fixed, so effort is your only variable.
Common faults
  • Swinging from the shoulders.
  • Elbows travelling forward at the top.
Archived — the dumbbell version, for when your replacements arrive

You sent your dumbbells back, so the program above is built for a bench and a backpack. This is the version that was here before, kept intact so nothing has to be rebuilt when a refund or replacement lands. Weekly volume was near-identical, so you can switch straight back without recalculating anything — restart at Phase 1 loads for a week to let your joints re-acclimatise to external weight, then pick up where your reps left off.

Session A — squat & flat press

16 working sets · ~55 min
1 Goblet squat RIR 2–3 › 3 × 8–12rest 2 minquads, glutes
Setup
  • Hold one dumbbell vertically against your chest, cupping the top head with both hands.
  • Feet a little wider than shoulders, toes turned out 15–30°.
  • Ribs down, brace as if about to be poked in the stomach.
Execution
  • Sit down between your hips, not back like a hinge. Knees track over your toes.
  • Go as deep as you can without the lower back rounding — thighs to parallel or below.
  • Drive up through the whole foot. Elbows stay inside the knees.
Common faults
  • Heels lifting. Fix by elevating heels on a 1–2 cm board, or widen the stance.
  • Chest collapsing forward as the dumbbell pulls you down. Squeeze the bell into your sternum harder.

When 50 lb gets easy: hold two dumbbells at your shoulders instead (front rack, up to 100 lb total), then elevate your heels, then move to the split-squat family. This is the first lift you'll outgrow — expect it around month 3.

2 Dumbbell bench press RIR 2 › 3 × 8–12rest 2–3 minchest, triceps, front delts
Setup
  • Sit on the end of the bench, dumbbells on your thighs. Lie back and kick them up one at a time.
  • Feet flat and planted. Shoulder blades pulled down and back into the bench.
  • Slight arch in the lower back is fine; ribcage stays down.
Execution
  • Lower until your upper arms are roughly level with your torso — feel the stretch across the chest.
  • Elbows at about 45° from your sides, not flared to 90°.
  • Press up and slightly together. Don't clank the bells at the top.
Common faults
  • Shoulders rolling forward off the bench at the bottom — you've gone too deep for your current mobility.
  • Bouncing out of the bottom. The stretched position is where the growth is; own it.

Recall substitute: until your dumbbells are replaced, do this as a floor press — same setup lying on the floor, upper arms stop when your triceps touch down. Shorter range, but nothing travels over your face.

3 One-arm dumbbell row RIR 2 › 3 × 10–12 / siderest 90 slats, mid-back, biceps
Setup
  • Opposite hand and knee on the bench, back flat and roughly parallel to the floor.
  • Working arm hanging straight down, shoulder allowed to reach toward the floor.
  • Neck neutral — look at the floor a foot ahead of the bench, not up.
Execution
  • Start by pulling the shoulder blade down and back, then bend the elbow.
  • Pull toward your hip, not your armpit. Elbow finishes past your ribs.
  • Lower all the way until the shoulder reaches again. That stretch is the point.
Common faults
  • Twisting the torso to help the weight up. If you're rotating, it's too heavy.
  • Stopping short at the bottom, turning it into a half-rep. Full reach every time.
4 Dumbbell Romanian deadlift RIR 2–3 › 3 × 10–12rest 2 minhamstrings, glutes
Setup
  • Dumbbells in front of your thighs, feet hip-width, knees softly bent and kept there.
  • Shoulders back, spine long from tailbone to skull.
Execution
  • Push your hips backward — the bells slide down your legs, staying in contact.
  • Stop when you feel a strong hamstring stretch or the back starts to round, usually mid-shin.
  • Drive the hips forward to stand. Squeeze the glutes at the top; don't lean back.
Common faults
  • Turning it into a squat. The knee angle should barely change from start to finish.
  • Letting the dumbbells drift away from the legs, which loads the lower back instead.

When 100 lb gets easy: stand on a low step or plate so you can reach further into the stretch before switching to the single-leg version in Session C.

5a Dumbbell lateral raise RIR 1–2 › 2 × 12–15supersetside delts
Setup
  • Stand tall, light dumbbells at your sides, tiny bend in the elbows.
  • Go much lighter than feels dignified. 10–20 lb is a real working weight here.
Execution
  • Raise out to the sides to shoulder height, leading with the elbows.
  • Lower under control over about two seconds. No swinging.
Common faults
  • Shrugging — the traps take over. Keep the shoulders pressed down.
  • Heaving with the hips. If you're bouncing, drop 5 lb.
5b Incline dumbbell curl RIR 1–2 › 2 × 10–15supersetbiceps, long head
Setup
  • Bench at 45–60°. Sit back against it and let both arms hang straight down, behind the line of your torso.
  • Palms forward, shoulders relaxed down. Don't let the shoulders creep forward to shorten the hang.
  • Go lighter than you would standing — the stretched start makes the same weight much harder.
Execution
  • Curl up without letting the upper arm travel forward; only the elbow moves.
  • Lower all the way back to a dead hang and let the biceps lengthen fully before the next rep.
  • Alternate arms or do both together — no meaningful difference.
Common faults
  • Shrugging the shoulder forward at the bottom, which removes the stretch that makes this version worth doing.
  • Cutting the bottom short. The dead hang is the exercise.

Why incline rather than standing: lying back puts your arm behind your torso, so the long head of the biceps starts fully lengthened instead of slack. That's the same stretched-position principle behind rung 4 of the Load Ladder. Worth being straight about the evidence: the principle is well supported, but the specific head-to-head against standing curls is not from a strong trial. Standing curls are not wrong — they just let you use momentum, and you own a bench that removes the temptation.

Superset: alternate 5a and 5b back to back with ~60 s between pairs. They don't compete for the same muscles, so this costs nothing and saves about six minutes.

Session B — split squat & incline press

16 working sets · ~55 min
1 Bulgarian split squat RIR 2–3 › 3 × 8–12 / siderest 2 minquads, glutes
Setup

  • Dumbbells hanging at your sides. Start with bodyweight only for the first week.

Phase 2 progression — decline weighted sit-up. Phase 1 sits deliberately below the optimum, and that is the point. The 10–20 set band comes from studies of trained lifters. 04 The ceiling

What to do when bodyweight stops being heavy

Without dumbbells you can't just put another plate on, so progression has to come from somewhere else. The good news is that you have more levers than a dumbbell gives you, not fewer — a backpack, your own leverage, your limb count, and your range of motion. When a movement hits the top of its rep range for two sessions running, walk down this ladder one rung at a time. Don't skip ahead.

1Add repsWork up to the top of the prescribed range. Free, and it's most of your first few months.
2Add a setGoing from 2 sets to 3 produces more growth than taking an existing set closer to failure. Cheaper than heroics.
3Load the backpackBooks, water bottles (a 1-litre bottle is a kilo), rice, sand. Pack heavy items low and tight against the back panel so nothing shifts mid-rep. This is your closest equivalent to adding a plate — use it until the straps or your patience run out.
4Change the leverageThe lever you have that dumbbells never gave you. Elevate your feet in a push-up, walk your feet forward in a row, raise your hips in a pike. Each one shifts more of your bodyweight onto the working muscle, and the adjustment is continuous rather than in 5 lb jumps.
5Go single-limbOne leg or one arm halves the load requirement instantly. A 2025 meta-analysis found no significant hypertrophy difference between unilateral and bilateral training — this is a lateral move, not a downgrade.
6Deepen the stretchHands on books for a deficit push-up; front foot elevated in a lunge. Lengthened-range work punches above its weight, and it's nearly free.
7Pause at the bottomTwo seconds in the stretched position kills the bounce and forces the muscle to restart the rep cold.
8Slow the lowering, or 1.5 reps2–4 seconds down, or a full rep followed by a half rep in the bottom half. Worth being precise: rep durations from 0.5 to 8 seconds produce similar growth. A slow eccentric doesn't add growth by itself — it makes a lighter load reach failure sooner, which is exactly the problem you have. Don't go past 8 seconds; beyond 10 it's clearly worse.

Rungs 4 and 5 are where this program actually lives. Leverage and single-limb work are what replace the weight you don't have, and they're genuinely effective rather than a consolation prize. Your legs will get there fastest, because a loaded backpack runs out long before a leg does.

Cardio

Lifting is not cardio, and the health returns from cardiovascular fitness are too large to skip. In a study of 122,007 people, cardiorespiratory fitness was inversely associated with mortality with no observed upper limit — the most fit group had roughly 80% lower all-cause mortality than the least fit. There is no point at which you're fit enough for it to stop paying.

Zone 2 Easy

150–180 min

Per week, across 3–4 sessions. Brisk walking, easy cycling, slow jogging. The test is conversation: you can talk in full sentences but wouldn't want to sing. Put it on non-lifting days or after lifting, never before.

Zone 5 Hard — optional

1–2 × week

The Norwegian 4×4: four rounds of 4 minutes hard, 3 minutes easy, about 38 minutes with warm-up. Reported to raise VO₂max roughly 7–10% in 8 weeks. Add it from Phase 2, not week 1.

Daily steps

7,000–8,000

The biggest lever you have that isn't exercise. Around 7,000 steps is where the mortality curve bends sharply, and step count is the best practical proxy for the calories you burn just living. Free, and it doesn't interfere with recovery.

The floor to aim for, from the WHO: 150–300 minutes of moderate activity a week plus muscle-strengthening on 2 or more days. This program clears both comfortably.

06 Staying in it

Recovery, and what to do when life happens

Sleep is a body-composition variable, not just rest. In a controlled trial, people on an identical 14-day calorie deficit sleeping 5.5 hours versus 8.5 hours lost a similar amount of total weight — but the short-sleep group lost 60% more lean mass and 55% less fat. Same diet. Same deficit. Different body.

Aim for 7–9 hours. If you can only fix one thing on this page and you're already training, fix this one — it changes what your deficit is made of.

Soreness

Expect real soreness in weeks 1–3, especially in the days after your first two sessions. It fades as you adapt and it is not a scoreboard — a session that leaves you wrecked is not better than one that doesn't. Soreness mostly comes from doing too much too soon, which is exactly why Phase 1 starts light. Keep moving on sore days; walking helps more than sitting still.

Deloads

Every 6–8 weeks, or any time you fail to hit your numbers two sessions running, take a lighter week: same sessions, same exercises, roughly two-thirds of the weight, stop 4–5 reps short on every set. You will come back stronger than you left.

A bad month is a maintenance month, not a reset. When work or travel wrecks your schedule, don't stop — shrink. Training at roughly one-third of your normal volume, as long as the intensity stays high, was enough to maintain strength and muscle size for six months. One session a week of two sets per lift will hold nearly everything you've built.

On missing a session. In the best real-world study of habit formation, it took a median of 66 days to make a new behaviour automatic — with an enormous range of 18 to 254 days depending on the person and the complexity of the habit. More usefully: the researchers found that missing a single opportunity did not measurably impair the habit forming at all.

One missed session costs you one session. What costs you the year is deciding that the missed session means you've failed.

What to log

Notebook, notes app, spreadsheet — the format doesn't matter, the existence does. For each set record exercise, variation, reps, RIR — and pack weight if you used it. Variation is the one that matters most here: "push-up, feet on bench, 3 × 10, 1 RIR" tells you what to beat, where "push-ups 3 × 10" tells you nothing. Without it you can't apply progressive overload, because you won't know what you did last time. Separately, every four weeks: waist measurement at the navel, photos in the same spot and light, and your top set on each main lift.

Why these numbers

Protein

1.6–2.2 g/kg

A meta-analysis of 49 studies and 1,863 people found gains kept improving up to about 1.62 g/kg/day and flattened after, with the confidence interval reaching 2.2. In a deficit, aim at the top of that range — protein is what stops weight loss from becoming muscle loss.

Spread it out

0.4–0.55 g/kg

Per meal, across 3–5 meals. The synthesis response to a meal runs about 2–2.5 hours and needs roughly 2.5–3 g of leucine to fire properly. 160 g of protein in two meals does less than the same 160 g in four.

Deficit ceiling

10–20%

Below maintenance. Go harder and you start paying in muscle: each extra 100 kcal/day of deficit reduces the lean-mass effect, and around 500 kcal/day is where lean gains are predicted to stop entirely. Patience here is not optional, it's mechanical.

Fibre

28–34 g

Roughly 14 g per 1,000 kcal. Fewer than one in ten adults hit this. It's the cheapest satiety you can buy, which matters enormously in Phase 3 — a fed feeling is what makes a deficit survivable.

Why whole food, without the moralising. In a tightly controlled inpatient trial, people were given either ultra-processed or unprocessed meals matched for calories presented, macronutrients, sugar, sodium and fibre, and told to eat as much or as little as they wanted. On the ultra-processed diet they ate 508 kcal/day more and gained weight.

No food is forbidden here. The argument for cooking most of your own food is simply that it makes hitting your target easier — you eat less without trying. That's a tool, not a virtue.

09 Everything else

Supplements, and what to ignore

Worth it Creatine monohydrate

3–5 g/day

The International Society of Sports Nutrition calls it the most effective legal supplement available for increasing high-intensity capacity and lean mass. Safe up to 30 g/day for five years in healthy people. The cramping and dehydration stories are not supported by evidence. Any time of day; no loading phase needed. Buy the cheapest monohydrate you can find.

Optional Protein powder

convenience

Useful if you struggle to hit your protein target from food, useless if you don't. It has no special properties. Whey, casein or plant blends all work.

Careful Caffeine

stop 8h before bed

Genuinely improves training performance. But 400 mg taken six hours before bed still cut objectively measured sleep by more than an hour. Given what sleep does for body composition, an afternoon coffee is a worse trade than it feels like.

Costly Alcohol

−24% MPS

After training, alcohol cut muscle protein synthesis by 24% even when taken with protein, and 37% with carbohydrate. It doesn't have to be zero. It should not be on training nights.

Things you can stop thinking about

  • Spot reduction. A meta-analysis of 13 studies and over 1,100 people found localised muscle training had no effect on localised fat. A 2023 trial did find a modest regional effect from abdominal endurance work, so "largely a myth" is the honest phrase — but either way, you cannot choose where fat comes off, so don't build a plan around it.
  • "Toning" as a separate kind of training. There is no toning stimulus. What people mean by toned is muscle plus low enough body fat to see it — which is Phase 2 followed by Phase 3. High reps with light weights is not a different mode, just a different point on the same curve.
  • Fasted cardio. Over a day, it doesn't change fat loss. Do it if you prefer it, skip it if you don't.
  • Detoxes and cleanses. Your liver and kidneys already do this.
  • Free weights versus machines. You have dumbbells. The argument is irrelevant to you, and it was always overstated.
  • Muscle confusion. Your muscles cannot be confused. Adaptation comes from progressive overload, which requires repeating movements long enough to get better at them.

12 Jargon

Every term on this site, in one place

Training writing is full of shorthand that nobody ever defines. Here is all of it.

RIR — reps in reserve
How many more reps you could have done when you stopped. "2 RIR" means you stopped with 2 left in the tank. It's a way to describe effort without needing to know your one-rep max.
Progressive overload
The work has to get harder over time. It's the one rule with no exceptions, and everything else on this site is a method for achieving it.
Double progression
Two things advance in turn: first reps, then difficulty. Climb to the top of the rep range, then make it harder and fall back to the bottom. Repeat forever.
Volume
Total hard sets per muscle per week. The main dial for how much muscle you build. 10–20 is the productive range for a trained lifter.
Unilateral
One limb at a time — a split squat rather than a squat. Halves the load you need for the same effect, which is why it matters so much without heavy weights.
Eccentric / concentric
The lowering half of a rep and the lifting half. The eccentric is where most soreness comes from, and controlling it is most of good technique.
Lengthened position
The point in a rep where the working muscle is most stretched — the bottom of a push-up, the bottom of a curl. Evidence suggests it drives a disproportionate share of growth.
Deficit (range)
Elevating your hands or foot so you can travel further than the floor normally allows. Confusingly unrelated to a calorie deficit.
Deload
A deliberately easy week — same sessions, about two-thirds the effort — taken every 6–8 weeks or when progress stalls. You come back stronger than you left.
Superset
Two exercises done back to back with little rest. Used here only for muscles that don't compete, so it saves time without costing performance.
Failure
The point where you cannot complete another rep with good form. You rarely need to go there — 0–2 reps short is enough, and much easier to recover from.
DOMS
Delayed-onset muscle soreness, the ache that peaks a day or two after training. Normal early on. Not a measure of whether a session worked.
BMR
Basal metabolic rate — the calories you'd burn doing nothing at all. The floor your daily total is built on.
TDEE
Total daily energy expenditure — everything you burn in a day, including moving around. Your maintenance number.
Deficit / surplus
Eating below or above maintenance. A deficit loses fat, a surplus builds tissue faster. This plan uses small versions of both, in sequence.
Recomp
Losing fat and gaining muscle at the same time. Unusual for trained lifters, reliable for beginners — which is why your scale weight will barely move at first.
MPS
Muscle protein synthesis — the process that turns protein you've eaten into muscle. It runs for roughly 2–2.5 hours after a meal, which is why protein gets spread across the day.
Leucine
The specific amino acid that switches muscle protein synthesis on. Roughly 2.5–3 g per meal is the practical target, and any decent protein serving clears it.
NEAT
All the energy you burn that isn't exercise — walking, fidgeting, standing. Usually a bigger lever on fat loss than training itself, and steps are the easiest way to track it.
Zone 2
Easy aerobic effort — you can hold a conversation but wouldn't want to sing. Most of your cardio should live here.
VO₂max
The maximum oxygen your body can use during hard exercise. The single best-evidenced predictor of how long you'll live.
1RM
One-rep max — the heaviest you could lift once. Used to describe training intensity. You never need to actually test it.
Hypertrophy
Muscle growth. Distinct from strength, which is partly a skill — you can gain one without much of the other.
EMG
A measurement of electrical activity in a muscle. Useful for comparing exercises, but a weaker form of evidence than actually measuring growth over months.

Where every number came from

Safety
  • CPSC — BowFlex adjustable dumbbell recall, 5 June 2025 — unit count, injury count, remedy.
  • BowFlex recall registration page.
Training guidelines
  • ACSM resistance training position stand, 2026 — first update in 17 years; removal of minimum-dose rules.
  • WHO guidelines on physical activity and sedentary behaviour, 2020 — 150–300 min moderate activity, 2+ strengthening days.
Volume, effort, rest, range of motion
  • Pelland et al. — resistance training dose-response meta-regressions — 67 studies, 2,058 participants; diminishing returns with volume.
  • Refalo et al. 2023 — proximity to failure meta-analysis — 15 studies; 0–4 RIR comparable; adding sets beats added proximity.
  • Schoenfeld et al. 2016 — interset rest periods — 3 min beat 1 min for strength and hypertrophy over 8 weeks.
  • Schoenfeld et al. 2015 — repetition duration meta-analysis — 0.5–8 s similar; >10 s inferior.
  • Schoenfeld et al. 2017, JSCR — low- vs high-load resistance training — with sets taken to failure, hypertrophy is similar across loads; only 1RM strength significantly favours heavy. The basis for training productively without weights.
  • Kikuchi & Nakazato 2017 — low-load bench press vs push-up — 8 weeks, load matched at 40% 1RM: pectoralis +18.3% (push-up) vs +19.4% (bench press), triceps +9.5% vs +10.3%; differences not statistically significant.
  • Lengthened partial repetitions vs full ROM, 2025 — similar adaptations; prioritise the stretched position.
  • Unilateral vs bilateral resistance training meta-analysis, Sports Medicine — 9 studies from 703 screened; no significant hypertrophy difference, strength gains follow specificity.
  • Barnett et al., JSCR — pectoralis EMG across bench angles, and a 12-variation bench press EMG comparison — small (~1–5%) lower-pec differences for decline; no significant chest-activation difference across flat, +25° and −25° at 6RM. Basis for not programming a decline press.
Cardio and activity
  • Mandsager et al. 2018, JAMA Network Open — 122,007 patients; no upper limit to fitness benefit; ~80% lower mortality in the elite group.
  • Paluch et al. — daily steps and all-cause mortality, 15 cohorts — benefit plateaus around 6,000–10,000 depending on age.
  • Norwegian 4×4 interval protocol — structure and reported VO₂max response.
Nutrition
  • ISSN position stand: protein and exercise (Jäger et al. 2017).
  • Morton et al. 2018 — protein supplementation meta-analysis — 49 studies, 1,863 participants; breakpoint ~1.62 g/kg/day, CI to ~2.2.
  • Protein quantity and distribution on body composition, 2024 — 0.4–0.55 g/kg per meal across 3–5 meals.
  • Pre-sleep protein and the muscle adaptive response — ~40 g casein; 22–33% higher overnight synthesis.
  • Hall et al. 2019, Cell Metabolism — inpatient RCT; +508 kcal/day on the ultra-processed arm despite matched macros and fibre.
  • Resistance training and high-quality weight loss — lean-mass retention during deficit; deficit-size effect on lean mass.
  • Dietary Guidelines for Americans — fibre 22–34 g/day, ~14 g per 1,000 kcal.
Recovery, supplements, habit
  • Sleep restriction and weight-loss composition (Nedeltcheva et al.) — 5.5 h vs 8.5 h; 60% more lean mass lost, 55% less fat.
  • ISSN position stand: creatine (Kreider et al. 2017) — 3–5 g/day; safety to 30 g/day for 5 years.
  • Drake et al. 2013, J Clin Sleep Med — 400 mg caffeine 6 h before bed reduced measured sleep by over an hour.
  • Parr et al. 2014, PLoS One — alcohol reduced myofibrillar protein synthesis 24% with protein, 37% with carbohydrate.
  • Bickel et al. — exercise dosing to retain training adaptations — one-third maintenance volume preserved strength and size for 6 months.
  • Lally & Gardner — making health habitual — median 66 days to automaticity, range 18–254; a single missed opportunity did not impair formation.
  • University of Sydney — spot reduction, and the 2023 abdominal endurance RCT that complicates it.
